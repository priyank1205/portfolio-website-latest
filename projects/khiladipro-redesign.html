The short version

01 / The gig

Khiladipro scores sports drills with a phone camera and visual AI. For the summer holidays it launched a nationwide, paid olympiad for students aged 12-18. I was the sole product designer.

02 / What I shipped

The landing and registration experience on web, and first-run onboarding plus every live event state on Android and iOS. No design system, eight weeks, a four-person launch team.

03 / The hard part

Parents paid before installing anything. Teenagers set up a camera alone. And an AI referee had to feel fair from three metres away.

04 / What happened

After the olympiad, the client came back with a second brief: an IPL fan campaign. A rehire is the outcome I can publish with confidence.

The brief

The hardest screen wasn’t the exercise. It was the moment before someone trusted us with their child, their card, and their camera.

Khiladipro was taking its camera-scoring technology from a training app to a first-of-its-kind national event. My job was to turn that idea into one coherent experience, from the first ad click to a child’s final point total.

Marketing could make the olympiad exciting. But a parent still needed a reason to pay before installing anything; a teenager had to set up a phone without help; and the product had to make AI scoring feel fair from three metres away.

ConstraintNo design system, a locked event calendar, and a four-person launch team.

The experience map

This wasn’t a funnel. It was a relay.

Every handoff had to preserve confidence: from a parent’s browser to a child’s room, then from a physical drill back into a digital score. Each stage kept one promise alive: “this is going to work in my home.”

01 / 04 · Believe

The conversion layer

Sell the thing before the app exists.

The parent pays before their child has tried the product. So the landing page had to do more than announce an event: it had to make the format believable, build desire, and answer the inevitable “how does this work?” quickly.

For competitors

Recognition, prizes, a reason to show up.

Make the payoff feel immediate.

For parents

A credible event they can feel good paying for.

Place legitimacy next to the decision.

For academies

A program that extends their coaching culture.

Signal social proof and scale.

How I got there

Three passes, three different questions.

02 / 04 · Commit

The conviction layer

Keep the reassurance in the form, not in a forgotten FAQ.

The conversion detail

The moment a parent hesitates is the moment they reach the payment step of a new, unfamiliar event. So I used an uncluttered three-step form and kept testimonial proof from coaches and athletes beside it, right where the doubt happens.

03 / 04 · Set up

The confidence layer

Teach the ritual before the score counts.

A first-time competitor has to prop their phone up, understand why it needs a camera, walk away from it, and trust that a machine will count fairly. I designed the onboarding around making each of those asks feel earned.

A

Explain the magic before the mechanism.

A short visual explainer gives the AI a human-scale purpose before the permission prompt appears.

B

Move from instruction to rehearsal.

A practice drill lets the child understand the feedback loop before their first scored attempt ever matters.

The rest of the path

Make the first-run flow easy to leave, and worth remembering.

Trust is not a single permission prompt. It is the feeling that the product knows when to explain, when to get out of the way, and how to end on encouragement.

Walk the complete onboarding flow ↗

The 3-metre rule

Design for the far edge of the room.

The camera needs a full body in frame, which puts the phone about three metres from the player. Past that line, nobody can touch the interface: feedback, instructions, and the rep count have to be readable from across the room.

Phone3mPlayer
Room-scale UI

Big enough to coach. Quiet enough to focus.

The interaction moves out of thumb range, so hierarchy, contrast, and motion do the work of tap targets.

04 / 04 · Return

The habit layer

Make every state feel like the event has started.

Event products spend most of their time between moments of action. The job was not just designing an active olympiad; it was making waiting, error, progress, and completion states carry the energy of the event.

State strategy

  1. 01
    Before

    Make anticipation informative, not empty.

  2. 02
    During

    Lead with points and the next test.

  3. 03
    After

    End each attempt on a small win.

The reliability work

Don’t leave users alone between the hero moments.

The event had to stay useful when a code failed, while a participant waited, and before a child committed to the next test.

The honest scorecard

I didn’t have access to client-owned analytics, so I won’t manufacture a finish line. Instead, this is the scorecard I would use to evaluate the design in production.

What I’d measure

What I’d improve next
