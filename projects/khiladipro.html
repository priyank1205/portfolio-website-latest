The challenge

The technology worked.
The experience had to earn trust.

KhiladiPro was taking its camera-based scoring technology into a paid, national sports olympiad. The challenge was making an unfamiliar format feel worth paying for—and possible to complete at home.

What I owned

Experience structure, responsive web, registration, first-run onboarding, and event interactions. I worked with a PM, engineer, and CEO inside an existing app language and a fixed summer launch window.

My design priority

Get a family from “Is this for us?”
to “My first drill counted.”

Registration creates the commitment.
A successful first drill delivers on it.

01

Conversion
Product framing · Information architecture

Before asking for money,
answer the right questions.

The competitor, the parent, and the academy coach arrived on the same page. I structured it around their different reasons to say yes, then carried that reassurance into registration.

The decision

Lead with the event.
Explain the technology later.

A teenager is entering a competition, not buying a scoring engine. Dates, prizes, and eligibility make the offer concrete; the format and steps help a parent or coach judge whether it will work.

  1. 1
    A reason to participate

    Put the olympiad and its limited window first.

  2. 2
    A reason to trust

    Give parents their own value proposition and a clear age range.

  3. 3
    A path to action

    Explain register → compete → win before asking people to navigate elsewhere.

On mobile, the primary action kept priority and the parent’s benefit moved below the fold. That preserved a clear entry point, but weakened immediate reassurance. I’d test parent comprehension here first.

The tradeoff & next testGuidance adds steps. Skipping adds risk.+

Why keep a rehearsal?

The nine-screen flow adds time before competition, but it introduces the camera, placement, and scoring separately. A skip option gives returning users a way through; it also means unfamiliar users can bypass useful preparation.

What I would validate

Observe actual 12–18-year-olds setting up in their own rooms. Can they position the phone without help, understand a missed rep, and complete a scoreable first drill? That is a stronger activation check than tutorial completion alone.

A competition is more than the attempt itself. I designed the time around it: gaining access, waiting for the event, choosing the next test, and checking earned points.

Working within constraints

The existing app had a visual language, but no formal design system. I used a consistent hierarchy for instructions, feedback, and next actions so the event felt coherent across its states.

Coverage & gapsWhat surrounds the happy path—and what still needs work+

The next gap I’d close: poor-network and failed-upload recovery for drill videos. A paid competition needs a clear answer when someone does the work but the system cannot record it.

The outcome

A launch delivered.
A relationship continued.

Rehired for a second engagement

After the olympiad shipped, KhiladiPro’s growth team brought me back to design the landing page for an IPL fan campaign.

The first engagement covered the web journey, first-run onboarding, and the event experience. The follow-up covered the campaign website on desktop and mobile; the in-house team owned its app screens.

Evidence, with its limits

The rehire is a signal of trust.
Product impact still needs data.

The client retained analytics access, so I can’t quantify conversion, activation, or retention. For a next engagement, I’d agree on measurement access at kickoff.

What I would measure

AcquisitionVisitor → paid registration

Segment by audience and device.

ActivationFirst scoreable drill completed

Track setup drop-offs and failed attempts.

ParticipationReturn to the next test

Pair return rate with unscoreable submissions.

Looking backThe two things I’d bring into the process earlier+

Test in the real context.

I’d prioritize comprehension sessions with the youngest participants, on their phones and in their rooms. A walkthrough at a desk cannot establish that feedback is readable while moving.

Challenge assumptions in the form.

I’d revisit the binary gender field and verify what information the competition actually needs. A familiar field is still a product decision that deserves a reason.

What this project taught me

The experience isn’t complete
until the promise works at home.

This project connected the work I care about most: framing the problem, choosing what matters, and carrying those decisions into the details people actually use.
