Product models 01 / 05

When the model changes, the first move changes

Each pivot changed how a game began, and so the first screens a player touched. These are the core flows I designed for each model, in the order they happened.

The model I joined

Play until the tournament closes.

Players paid gems to enter time-bounded casual-game tournaments, played as often as they liked before close, and the top three split the pool. The product was not near product-market fit.

Pivot 01 Late 2019

Wait for a game to fill

Players searched for opponents, pooled their entry fees and played synchronously. The winner took the pool. I designed the path from finding a match to seeing the result: final designs that shipped. Scroll to walk the four stages, or step through them.

We ran Casual, Card and Trivia. Trivia worked best; the others less so.

The structural constraint: enough compatible players had to be searching at the same moment.

Pivot 02 Instant, later cash tables

Join a game already in progress

That liquidity problem, and a market where most Indian real-money spend went to poker and rummy, led to a different format. About six seats: sit with money, join from the next hand, play for stakes with rake per hand, leave when you like. I designed the sit flow and the buy-in.

A branching entry

From flow to interaction

Keep the amount above the thumb.

On press, the value label lifts above the slider, so the amount stays readable while the player adjusts the buy-in. Beside it, the wallet: the money the player sits with.

Cash tables became the main product and revenue format for most of my tenure, across poker, rummy and casual games. Trivia was retired from the cash-table format.

Added alongside

Tournaments brought players to the tables

Once cash tables were the core, we added multi-table tournaments. They drew strong participation and funnelled players into cash tables. I designed the tournament surface, including a details page with a state for each stage.

Reasons to return 03 / 05

Progress today. A reason to return tomorrow.

I watched players stream on YouTube and Twitch, followed how they moved between products, and played extensively across real-money platforms. Players went where the tournaments were good and the opponents beatable. The product team’s response was a loyalty programme of gameplay-tied tasks. I designed it end to end.

  1. 01A task worth returning toThe hero task swaps on completion by a programmed order; each card carries progress, reward and claim.

  2. 02A longer progression20, 50, 200 and 500 hands: claimed, claimable and in progress share one ladder.

  3. 03A reason to return tomorrowOne reward paid across seven daily claims; today’s claim and the next unlock stay visible.

A second mechanism

Scratch cards with a place and a time.

I conceptualised the scratch-card system: tiers, unlock times and expiry, with a card awarded at the deposit-success moment.

What I’d do differently

Three things I took forward.

  • Build the component system earlierThe thirteen-state card came late.
  • Brief retention and responsible play togetherThey belong in the same design problem.
  • Write decisions down as we make themToo much of the context lived in my head.
