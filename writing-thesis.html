The far side of the room

A phone camera refereed a paid sports olympiad. Designing it meant designing for someone who could not reach the screen.

Priyank AgarwalFrom the KhiladiPro case study
First, where the phone goes

Most screens are designed for the length of an arm. You hold the phone, you read it, you tap it. KhiladiPro’s were not. It ran a paid sports olympiad for 12 to 18 year olds, played at home and scored by a phone camera. A player propped the phone up, stepped back, and did the drill while the camera counted.1

That one physical fact changed almost every decision I made on the product.

The referee is a phone

When a camera decides the score, trust has a physical shape. The count has to be readable from where the player stands, not from where the designer sits. So the count on the scored drill is set large, and each screen says one thing at a time.

A rehearsal before the drill that counts

If onboarding failed, the drill could not be scored, and in a paid event that becomes a refund request.2 So I designed onboarding as a rehearsal on the exact screen the scored test uses. The first time it counts is the second time they have seen it.

Where the phone goes

Before any of that, the phone has to find its place. It goes on a raised surface, facing the player, so its front camera sees the whole body. Then the player stands inside the guidelines until the camera has them, and the drill begins.

What I took from it

Three things have stayed with me. Design for the distance the screen will actually be read from. Rehearse the moment that counts, on the screen that will count it. And when someone cannot reach the screen to ask, say one thing at a time.

KhiladiPro shipped every screen, and brought me back for an IPL campaign page.3 The full story, with all nine onboarding screens, is in the case study.
